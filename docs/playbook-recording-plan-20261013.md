### What if I miss a workout?

HOOK (5–10s): What if I miss a workout?
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Leave the missed session behind and choose the next realistic slot. Do not squeeze two hard sessions into one day to catch up. Keep the remaining week manageable; if this keeps happening, ask your coach to adjust the schedule. Open your weekly planner. Identify one session you can actually attend, then discuss moving the missed session with your coach. A missed session is useful information about your schedule, not a debt. Tell your coach which deadline, commute or family commitment got in the way so the next week can be more realistic.
ACTION (10–20s): Open your weekly planner. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### The 20-minute emergency workout

HOOK (5–10s): The 20-minute emergency workout
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Use a shorter version of your assigned session, approved by your coach. Keep the warm-up and the important movements. Reduce extra work before rushing rests or technique. Preview the option before accepting; if none is approved, ask your coach. A time limit does not mean you need a new workout. Your coach can choose which assigned movements to retain and how many sets to perform. The tool shows that exact option, keeps your original session and records your choice. The displayed time is a planning window, not a promise that every person will finish at the same pace.
ACTION (10–20s): Preview a coach-approved workout option. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### Training during a 60–70-hour work week

RECORDING NOTES
Your week has blown up. What now?
Aim for 2–3 minutes.
VISUAL: Show the three capacity levels in the demanding-week lesson: Normal / Constrained / Survival. Point to one level at a time. The levels are a coaching framework, not scientific thresholds.

HOOK · Camera
You have a closing deadline, the calls are running over, and the week you planned on Sunday no longer exists. If that sounds familiar, here is what I want you to do: adjust the week before you write it off.

TEACH THE IDEA · Show the capacity diagram
Think of your week in three levels. A normal week is when the full plan genuinely fits. A constrained week is when you have less time, so we protect the most useful sessions and use the shorter options we have agreed. A survival week is when recovery, regular meals and safe movement need to come first. You are choosing the version you can actually execute.

EXPLAIN THE SCIENCE · Keep the diagram visible
Here is why that makes sense. Research shows that different resistance-training schedules can improve strength and muscle size. Maintenance studies also suggest some adaptations can be retained with less training, although that depends on the person and the outcome. That does not mean a tiny workout replaces everything. It means one difficult week does not require an all-or-nothing response.

WALK THROUGH A WORKDAY · Camera or calendar visual
Imagine you usually train Monday, Wednesday and Friday evenings. A deal closes on Friday and those evenings are now unreliable. You still have Tuesday morning and a Saturday window. Put those in the diary and agree which sessions matter most. Do not try to squeeze three workouts into Friday night and Saturday. And if evenings keep disappearing, we need a different timetable, not another promise to try harder.

GIVE ONE ACTION · Camera
Today, look at the next seven days. Choose the training windows that are genuinely available and one reliable meal for a late night. If the full plan does not fit, use an approved shorter option or tell me early so we can adjust it. A smaller week, planned deliberately, is still a week you can build on.

CLOSING ACTION: Choose two realistic training windows and one reliable meal fallback.

### Making progress with three sessions per week

HOOK (5–10s): Making progress with three sessions per week
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Three consistent sessions can give your coach plenty to work with. Follow the assigned movements, record the work and recover between sessions. Judge progress across weeks rather than adding extra sessions because one day felt easy. Frequency is one part of a programme alongside exercise selection, effort, volume and recovery. Your assigned three-session plan should reflect your goals and experience. Use the training log to show your coach what happened. If attendance is reliable but progress is unclear, review the plan together before increasing workload.

### What to do when the gym is busy

HOOK (5–10s): What to do when the gym is busy
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Check the alternatives your coach attached to the exercise. Use an approved option with equipment you can access. If there is no suitable alternative, move to another assigned movement or ask your coach; do not copy the original load onto unfamiliar equipment. A substitute should preserve the intent of the movement and suit your experience and limitations. Machines, cables and free weights can feel very different. Read the saved instructions and record the equipment you used so the coach can interpret your performance. Your existing exercise-options controls hold approved substitutions.

### Training while travelling

HOOK (5–10s): Training while travelling

### Eating well during late office nights

RECORDING NOTES
It is 9 pm and dinner has become a problem.
Aim for 2–3 minutes.
VISUAL: Show the office-meal diagram: Protein / Plants / Energy. Use meal examples, not fixed plate percentages. Refer to the client’s assigned portions and restrictions.

HOOK · Camera
It is nine o’clock, you have worked through lunch, and now you are trying to make a sensible dinner decision when you are hungry and exhausted. The useful change usually happens earlier in the day, not when you finally open the delivery app.

GIVE A USABLE STRUCTURE · Show the meal-components diagram
Start with a familiar protein source that fits your plan and dietary requirements. Add fruit or vegetables in a form you will actually eat. Then include carbohydrate and fats in portions that fit your assigned meal. This is a structure, not a rule that every plate needs to look identical. And it does not mean cutting out rice, bread or potatoes because you are eating late.

EXPLAIN THE SCIENCE · Point to the protein component
Protein supplies amino acids used in repair and training adaptation. Your total daily intake matters more than finding one special food. But a protein source alone does not make a meal suitable: portions, the rest of the day and your preferences still matter. We are making your existing plan easier to follow, not inventing a new target tonight.

WALK THROUGH AN EXAMPLE · Camera, then a written meal example
If you eat meat, a suitable chicken-and-rice meal with vegetables could work. If you do not, tofu with rice and vegetables could be an option. Those are examples, not personalised prescriptions. Check ingredients and portions against your plan. For allergies or kosher requirements, confirm the preparation and suitability with the venue; a menu title is not enough.

SOLVE THE TIMING PROBLEM · Camera
Now the part that makes this work: if you know at lunchtime that the evening will run late, arrange dinner then. Eat before the call if that is practical, or choose a trusted order in advance. If the meeting arrives unexpectedly, use that same fallback instead of searching twenty menus. Tonight, save one suitable order. Tomorrow, decide dinner before the late call starts. That is one less decision when your attention is already spent.

CLOSING ACTION: Save one suitable late-night order and decide when to use it.

### Ordering food at restaurants

Use your plan as a guide, not a requirement to calculate every bite during a client dinner. Review the actual menu when you can. Preparation, sauces and portions vary, so an app cannot guarantee a restaurant meal matches a saved recipe. If you have an allergy, speak to the restaurant directly about preparation and cross-contact. A busy dinner can still fit your routine; tell your coach about repeated situations that need a practical strategy.

### Nutrition during business travel

HOOK (5–10s): Nutrition during business travel

Return to your normal plan at the next convenient meal, without trying to compensate for the whole trip. Look at travel timing: early departure, airport wait, hotel arrival and client dinners. Where permitted and safely stored, an assigned portable meal can bridge a long gap. At an airport, assess actual options rather than relying on a fictional menu. Keep the trip workable and bring the recurring obstacles to your next check-in.

### What to do after overeating

HOOK (5–10s): What to do after overeating
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Return to your usual next meal. Avoid skipping food or adding punishment exercise to compensate. Notice what led up to it: a long gap, stress, alcohol or an unusually large portion. One meal does not require rewriting your targets. Review the circumstances without turning one event into a judgement about yourself. A practical change might be eating before a long meeting or keeping a backup meal available. If eating feels out of control, repeatedly distressing or involves compensatory behaviours, tell your coach and seek support from a qualified clinician. The app is not an eating-disorder treatment.
ACTION (10–20s): Open your meal plan. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### Meal preparation for busy professionals

HOOK (5–10s): Meal preparation for busy professionals
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Prepare one or two useful meals rather than an entire perfect week. Use your assigned recipes, make a realistic number of servings and plan safe storage. A reliable lunch and backup dinner can solve more problems than a complicated Sunday cooking session. Choose recipes you already enjoy and check your shopping list before cooking. Batch preparation changes the number of portions, not the serving size in your daily log. Label leftovers and follow local food-safety guidance for refrigeration, reheating and transport. If facilities are limited, ask for meals that fit those constraints.

### What to do after a poor night’s sleep

HOOK (5–10s): What to do after a poor night’s sleep
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Check how you feel before training. Use an approved tired-day option if appropriate, or ask your coach about moving the session. Avoid chasing a personal best when alertness or technique is poor. Protect your next sleep opportunity rather than trying to solve the day with more stimulants. One poor night and persistent sleep problems need different responses. Consider whether you can work, drive and train safely today. Tell your coach if fatigue is changing technique or recovery. Persistent unexplained tiredness, snoring with breathing pauses or repeated sleep disruption deserves medical advice, not a wearable-based diagnosis.
ACTION (10–20s): Preview a coach-approved workout option. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### Caffeine and energy management

HOOK (5–10s): Caffeine and energy management
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Notice when and how much caffeine you use, especially later in the day. If it disrupts sleep, move the last serving earlier and review the pattern. Do not use escalating caffeine to push through persistent fatigue. Sensitivity and sleep timing differ between people. Track the practical pattern rather than assuming a universal cut-off suits you. Consider caffeine in tea, coffee, energy drinks and some supplements. If reducing a large habitual intake, a gradual change may be easier. Pregnancy, medicines and heart symptoms need advice tailored by a qualified professional.
ACTION (10–20s): Save this guide. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### Managing stress during demanding weeks

HOOK (5–10s): Managing stress during demanding weeks
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Choose one small action you can do today: a short break, a walk, a regular meal or an earlier finish. Tell your coach what the week actually looks like. Coaching can help adjust routines, but persistent distress needs appropriate professional support. When everything feels urgent, reduce the number of decisions. Decide the next meal and the next achievable activity instead of redesigning the entire week. Use a check-in to describe the constraint and the support you need. Breathing or relaxation practices may help some people, but are optional and not a substitute for mental-health care.

### Recovery when work and training are demanding

HOOK (5–10s): Recovery when work and training are demanding
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Bring persistent fatigue or falling performance to your coach before adding more work. Protect regular meals, adequate sleep opportunity and planned easier days. Use how you feel and your training log together; a single wearable score should not dictate the programme. Review the recent pattern: attendance, effort, work demands, sleep and food availability. Your coach can decide whether volume or scheduling needs to change. Avoid treating soreness as a target or buying supplements to rescue an unworkable routine. Pain, illness or ongoing unexplained fatigue requires appropriate assessment.

### Why body weight fluctuates

RECORDING NOTES
The scale went up. Did you gain fat?
Aim for 2–3 minutes.
VISUAL: Open the weight-fluctuations lesson. Show the daily zigzag first, then click “Show the overall direction”. Explicitly label it an invented example, not client data or a forecast.

HOOK · Camera
You have had a good week, then you step on the scale after a restaurant meal and the number is up. It is very easy to think the week has gone backwards. But the scale measures your total body weight. It does not tell you how much body fat changed overnight.

EXPLAIN WHAT IS BEING WEIGHED · Show Water / Contents / Tissue
Part of that number is water. Changes in salt intake, carbohydrate storage and hormonal patterns can affect it. Part is the food and fluid still in your body. Longer-term changes can also include fat and lean tissue. So a higher reading is real, but the explanation is not automatically fat gain.

MAKE THE PATTERN VISIBLE · Show the daily zigzag, then reveal the gold direction line
Look at this example. These readings are invented to explain the idea; they are not your data. There are days when the line goes up, even though the overall direction is down. If you react to each upward day by cutting food or adding exercise, you can keep changing a plan that was already moving in the intended direction.

GIVE A BETTER WAY TO ASSESS · Camera
If weighing is useful for you, use comparable conditions and look at repeated observations. Also consider your training performance, waist measurements if appropriate, and how your clothes fit. Even a weight trend cannot perfectly separate fat, muscle and fluid. If fat loss is your goal and a longer pattern has flattened, we review the whole picture before changing your targets.

GIVE ONE ACTION · Camera
After one higher reading, keep your next meal normal. Note anything that changed, such as travel, a meal out or measurement timing, and bring the pattern to your check-in. If weighing makes you anxious or distressed, tell me; more measurements are not always more helpful. Rapid unexplained changes, swelling or other symptoms deserve medical advice. The aim is to use information well, rather than let one number run the day.

CLOSING ACTION: Keep the next meal normal and review comparable readings before changing the plan.

### What to do when fat loss stalls

HOOK (5–10s): What to do when fat loss stalls
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Review several weeks with your coach before changing food targets. Check whether meals, activity and measurements have been consistent enough to interpret. Bring travel, missed logs and schedule changes into the discussion. Avoid aggressive cuts based on a few readings. A plateau cannot be diagnosed from a single chart. Missing information can look like stalled progress, and measurement conditions can obscure a trend. Your coach should review goals, adherence, recovery and whether the plan remains appropriate. The Playbook explains the review process; it does not automatically reduce calories or add exercise.
ACTION (10–20s): Ask your coach. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### Understanding your progress

HOOK (5–10s): Understanding your progress
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Use the measures that match your goal. Attendance, strength, routine and recorded measurements can tell different parts of the story. Look for a repeatable pattern over time and ask your coach what matters most for the next phase. A chart is only as useful as the observations behind it. Check dates, missing entries and measurement conditions before comparing values. Do not compare your response with someone else’s highlight reel. Use your next check-in to agree one or two meaningful indicators instead of collecting every available number.
ACTION (10–20s): Review your progress. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### Understanding wearable information

HOOK (5–10s): Understanding wearable information
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Treat your watch as one source of context. Check when it last synced and whether readings are missing. Estimated calories and recovery scores are not exact measurements or diagnoses. Pair trends with how you feel and what your coach sees in the programme. Device brands calculate scores differently. Missing data is not zero, and combining overlapping step sources can double-count activity. Share only the readings you want your coach to see and check the actual receipt time in Devices & apps. A score cannot clear you to train through illness or pain; concerning symptoms need appropriate assessment.
ACTION (10–20s): Open Devices & apps. Bring the recurring obstacle to your next check-in.

### Why strength matters as you age

HOOK (5–10s): Why strength matters as you age
EXPLANATION (30–90s): A consistent strength routine can support everyday function as you get older. Start from the programme your coach assigned and progress at a manageable pace. The best starting point reflects your experience, health and equipment, rather than your age alone. Strength training involves learning movements and gradually practising them with appropriate resistance. It should be adapted around existing limitations and recovery. Your coach can review technique, attendance and progression. New pain or a medical restriction needs individual assessment; general guidance is not medical clearance.

### Building habits that survive demanding periods

HOOK (5–10s): Building habits that survive demanding periods
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Choose a small version of the routine that fits a difficult day. Tie it to a reliable moment, such as lunch or arriving home. Keep the decision simple and ask your coach which minimum actions are useful when the week becomes unpredictable. A fallback routine works best when prepared before the busy period. Decide a backup meal, a feasible activity slot and how to contact your coach. Review what actually happened instead of adding more rules. A habit is a practical cue and repeated action, not a streak you must protect at the expense of health.

### Getting back on track after a difficult week

HOOK (5–10s): Getting back on track after a difficult week
EXPLANATION (30–90s): Start with the next useful action: choose a meal, book an achievable session or send your check-in. You do not need a perfect Monday or a punishment week. Tell your coach what changed so the plan can match your current circumstances. Use the existing planner rather than creating an ambitious catch-up schedule. Check whether the original week still fits your work and recovery. Pick one commitment you can keep and build from there. If an injury, illness or significant life event disrupted the routine, let your coach know before restarting the previous workload.
